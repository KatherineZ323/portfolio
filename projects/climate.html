Who Is Responsible for Climate Change?

A scrollytelling data story examining CO₂ emissions through four lenses — revealing how the metric you choose reshapes the politics of climate responsibility.

Overview

This project was built for Cornell's Data Communication course (INFO 3312/5312). The challenge: take a contested, politically charged question — who is responsible for climate change? — and present it through data in a way that's honest, nuanced, and genuinely educational.

Rather than picking a single answer, we designed a scrollytelling experience that walks readers through four empirically valid lenses on CO₂ emissions, each of which produces a different ranking and a different verdict on national responsibility.

The Central Question

"If every nation at the negotiating table points to a different number to defend its position — and every number is technically correct — which one should we trust?"

The metric you choose is never just a technical decision. It reflects a deeper belief about what kind of fairness should govern climate responsibility. Our story is designed for anyone trying to read a climate headline, a UN negotiation brief, or a political speech more critically.

The Four Lenses

We examined seven major emitters — China, the US, India, Germany, the UK, Russia, and Japan — through four different measurement frameworks:

→ United States

🔄
Consumption

Each lens is technically correct — and each encodes a different definition of fairness. The story doesn't tell readers which metric is right, but makes them a more informed reader of the ones others choose to use.

Design Process

Narrative Structure

We structured the story as four chapters — one per lens — each following the same arc: introduce the metric, show China's or the US's position shift, then expose the limitation of that lens. The epilogue synthesizes all four into a framework for critical reading.

Scrollytelling Mechanics

Built with Quarto, the story uses scroll-triggered chart transitions to animate the shift between metrics. As readers scroll through each chapter, the visualization updates to show the same seven countries through a new lens — making the comparison visceral and immediate rather than abstract.

Visual Design Choices

We chose a restrained, editorial color palette and typography to signal seriousness and credibility. Each country was assigned a consistent color across all four charts, so readers could track a single nation's position as it shifts between lenses — reinforcing the story's core insight about metric-dependence.

Data Source

All data came from Our World in Data's CO₂ and Greenhouse Gas Emissions dataset (2024 release). We selected seven countries to represent a range of development trajectories, historical industrialization timing, and current emission levels.

Key Design Decisions

No single "correct" answer

The hardest design decision was resisting the temptation to editorialize. Every chart annotation, every transition, every piece of body copy was written to inform — not to advocate. This required multiple rounds of revision to strip out language that implied a verdict.

Audience: the critical reader

We explicitly designed for someone trying to read a climate headline or policy speech more skeptically, not for climate scientists. This shaped vocabulary choices, the depth of statistical explanation, and the decision to foreground "why this matters beyond the data" in the epilogue.

The consumption-production lens as the surprise

We deliberately positioned the consumption vs. production lens last, because it's the most counterintuitive and the most politically charged. The scroll pacing here is slower, giving readers more time to absorb the implication that deindustrialization can be an accounting artifact.

Outcomes & Reflection

4
Distinct data lenses, each telling a different story about the same emissions data
7
Countries analyzed across all four frameworks with consistent visual encoding

This project sharpened my thinking about how data visualization is never neutral — every design choice encodes an argument. The discipline of presenting contested data fairly, without flattening its complexity, is one I want to carry into product and UX work: interfaces that help users make informed decisions rather than nudging them toward a predetermined conclusion.
